Every robot launch comes with a spec table, and every spec table is a negotiation between the marketing team and the test lab. The numbers are rarely wrong. They are just measured under conditions that may have nothing to do with yours.

Here is what each headline figure actually means, and what to ask before you trust it.

## Payload is a number with an asterisk

Rated payload is the mass the arm can carry *at a stated speed, orientation and centre of gravity offset*. Move the load 100 mm further from the tool flange and the effective capacity can fall by a third. Ask for the payload–inertia diagram, not the single number.

Two more deductions people forget: the gripper is part of the payload, and so is the cabling and any sensor you bolt to the wrist.

## Repeatability is not accuracy

Repeatability is how tightly the robot clusters when it returns to the *same taught point*. Accuracy is how close it gets to a point specified in world coordinates. Industrial arms advertise repeatability because it is often ten times better than their accuracy.

> **Rule of thumb**
>
> If your process teaches positions by jogging the robot there once, repeatability is the number that matters. If positions come from CAD or from a vision system, you need accuracy — and you will probably need calibration to get it.

## The four figures, side by side

| Spec | Measured as | What it hides |

|---|---|---|

| Payload | Mass at rated speed and CoG offset | Falls off sharply with load offset; gripper mass counts against it. |

| Reach | Centre of joint 1 to tool flange | The far edge of the envelope is often unusable — singularities and reduced stiffness. |

| Repeatability | ISO 9283 scatter over repeated cycles | Says nothing about absolute positioning accuracy. |

| Duty cycle | Percentage of an hour at rated load | Thermal derating; continuous operation may be well below peak. |

## Cycle time is a claim about a path, not a robot

Published cycle times usually quote a standard pick-and-place move — a short vertical lift, a horizontal traverse, a descent. Real cells add blend radii, waypoints for obstacle avoidance, waits on sensor confirmation and conveyor sync. Budget generously.

## For mobile robots, add three more

- **Runtime versus duty runtime.** Idle runtime and runtime while driving a loaded shelf are different numbers.
- **Localisation drift.** Ask over what distance and in what environment — a feature-rich warehouse is the easy case.
- **Stop distance at rated speed.** This is the number your safety assessor will ask for, and it grows with load.

> A spec sheet tells you what the robot did once, in a room built for it. Your job is to work out what it will do every day, in yours.

## The questions to send back

Before you shortlist anything, ask the vendor for: the payload–inertia curve, the accuracy figure alongside repeatability, the duty-cycle derating chart, and a cycle time for *your* path rather than theirs. Vendors who have done the testing will send these without argument. That in itself is a signal.
