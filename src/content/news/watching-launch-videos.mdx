Launch videos are marketing, and that is fine — every industry produces them. The problem is that robotics videos are unusually easy to misread, because the difference between an autonomous system and a well-teleoperated one is nearly invisible at 1080p.

These are the things worth looking for, roughly in order of how much they tell you.

## 1. Count the cuts

An unbroken take is the single strongest signal in any robotics video. If a sixty-second reel never holds a shot for more than four seconds, the interesting question is what happens in second five. Continuous, wide, unedited footage is what serious teams publish when the system is ready.

## 2. Check the clock

Speed ramps are common and usually disclosed in small type. Look for human motion in frame: if a person walks past at an unnatural pace, the footage is sped up. A robot that looks fluid at 2× may look laboured at 1×, and 1× is the speed your process runs at.

> **Ask for the boring version**
>
> The most useful thing you can request from a vendor is unedited, fixed-camera footage of a full shift. Teams that have it will share it.

## 3. Look at the environment, not the robot

- **Floor.** Poured, level, uncluttered floors are a stage. Cable runs, ramps and pallets are reality.
- **Lighting.** Even, diffuse, shadowless light helps vision systems enormously and is rare on a real site.
- **Object placement.** Objects arranged with even spacing and consistent orientation have been staged for the perception stack.
- **Background.** A textured, feature-rich space is the easy case for localisation. A long white corridor is the hard one.

## 4. Find the humans

Look at the edges of frame and at reflections. An operator holding a controller, a safety observer with a hand near an e-stop, or a laptop on a cart with someone watching it all tell you about the level of supervision. None of these are damning — supervised autonomy is a legitimate product — but they should change how you read the claim.

## 5. Read what is not claimed

Press releases are drafted carefully. "Demonstrated" is not "deployed". "Pilot" is not "production". "Up to" is a ceiling, not a typical value. And a video with no stated success rate is telling you the success rate is not the story they want to lead with.

> Nobody films the ninety attempts before the one that worked. That is not dishonest. It just means the video is evidence of a best case, and you are buying an average.

## The one request that settles it

Ask to run your own part, on your own bench, with your own lighting, for a full day. Everything else is inference.
